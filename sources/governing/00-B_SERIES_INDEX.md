For a first-pass Canadian categories list, I would use:

Category — What we would look for

1. Legal identity, registration and membership — Indian Act registration/status, band membership, citizenship under self-government arrangements, and consequences attached to each classification

2. Land ownership and possession — Reserve land, Certificates of Possession, leases, surrender/designation, land codes, and differences from ordinary provincial real-property systems

3. Housing and matrimonial property — Family homes on reserve, possession after relationship breakdown, division of value, emergency occupation and interaction with First Nation laws

4. Wills, estates and inheritance — Who administers estates, wills, intestacy and succession involving property on reserve

5. Taxation — Income tax, GST/HST, provincial sales taxes, property taxation, First Nations taxes and the role of where income/property/transactions are situated

6. Credit, mortgages and seizure of property — Limits on pledging, mortgaging, attaching or seizing certain property situated on reserve and what that means for financing

7. First Nation government and elections — Indian Act elections, the First Nations Elections Act, custom election systems, council powers and self-government systems

8. Indigenous law-making and self-government — Areas in which Indigenous governments enact their own laws under land codes, self-government agreements, modern treaties or federal enabling legislation

9. Child and family services — Indigenous jurisdiction, notice to Indigenous governing bodies, placement priorities, cultural continuity and interaction among federal, provincial and Indigenous law

10. Education — On-reserve education, federal funding/administration, First Nation control of education and differences from ordinary provincial delivery

11. Health services and benefits — Distinct federal programs and jurisdictional arrangements for eligible First Nations and Inuit people, versus ordinary provincial health systems

12. Income assistance, housing and public services — On-reserve social assistance, housing, infrastructure and other services where federal/First Nation/provincial responsibilities differ

13. Criminal sentencing and corrections — Rules requiring particular attention to the circumstances of Aboriginal offenders, including what is commonly discussed through Gladue principles; this is a distinct sentencing rule, not a separate criminal code for Indigenous people. [Department of Justice Canada](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-46/section-718.2-20240720.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

14. Policing and administration of justice — Indigenous policing arrangements, community justice programs, enforcement authority and Indigenous justice institutions

15. Hunting, fishing and trapping — Aboriginal and treaty harvesting rights, licensing, food/social/ceremonial fisheries, commercial rights where established, and federal/provincial regulatory interaction

16. Natural resources — Forestry, minerals, water, wildlife and resource management where reserve lands, treaty lands, Aboriginal rights or Indigenous jurisdiction change the applicable regime

17. Environmental and impact assessment — Required consultation with affected Indigenous groups, consideration of impacts on section 35 rights and treatment/protection of Indigenous knowledge. The federal Impact Assessment Act expressly contains all three kinds of provisions. [Department of Justice Canada](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-2.75/section-12.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

18. Crown consultation and accommodation — Government decisions that may affect asserted or established Aboriginal or treaty rights, and the procedures that follow—

19. Treaties and modern treaties — Rules that differ because a historic treaty, modern treaty or land-claims agreement governs a particular Nation or territory

20. Business, licensing and economic activity on Indigenous lands — Business regulation, land use, permitting, taxation, economic-development corporations and jurisdiction on reserve/settlement lands

21. Infrastructure and local government functions — Roads, utilities, zoning, public works, service fees and local regulation under Indian Act, land-code or self-government regimes

22. Indigenous knowledge, information and data — Statutory treatment of Indigenous knowledge, confidentiality, disclosure, governance of community information and emerging data-governance rules

There are already some very strong paper families hiding inside that list. Taxation alone could split into retail sales tax, employment income, property tax, First Nations taxation powers, and excise taxes. Federal law expressly exempts certain reserve-situated property from taxation, while CRA guidance applies a connecting-factors analysis to some employment income; First Nations can also exercise particular taxation powers of their own. [Department of Justice Canada](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/i-5/page-8.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Property could become an equally rich series. Section 89 of the Indian Act restricts seizure and mortgage-type enforcement against certain reserve-situated property, while the Family Homes on Reserves and Matrimonial Interests or Rights Act exists specifically because ordinary provincial/territorial matrimonial-property law does not simply govern reserve land in the usual way. [Department of Justice Canada](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-5/section-89.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And governance is not one regime either. Depending on the First Nation, elections or land governance can operate through the Indian Act, dedicated federal legislation, a land code, a self-government agreement or another governing arrangement. The current First Nations Elections Act and Framework Agreement on First Nation Land Management Act are concrete examples. [Department of Justice Canada](https://www.laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/F-11.65/FullText.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The key insight for the pipeline is that this gives us two axes, not one:

Sector/category × legal trigger

The trigger column would be something like: registered status | band/First Nation membership | reserve location | Indigenous government jurisdiction | treaty/modern treaty | Aboriginal or treaty right | Indigenous identity specifically recognized by statute.

That second axis prevents us from accidentally writing “Indigenous people get X” when the actual law says something much narrower, such as “a registered person whose property is situated on reserve,” or something much broader, such as a provision concerning Indigenous peoples under section 35.

So I think this is the right front end. We can now turn these 22 categories into a topic bank, without advocating anything yet. The next pass would be to break each category into concrete, researchable questions of the form “When does the ordinary rule change, for whom, where, and under what authority?” That is where the genuinely interesting papers should start appearing.
